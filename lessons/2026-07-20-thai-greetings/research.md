## 1. Core Vocabulary and Phrases

### 1.1 Hello / basic greeting

| Thai | RTGS | Phonetic (tones) | Tones | Literal | Usage notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| สวัสดี | sawatdi | sà-wàt-dii | L-L-M | "well-being, auspiciousness" | Universal greeting AND farewell. Time-agnostic: works morning, noon, night. Never said bare in polite speech — always + ครับ/ค่ะ. |
| สวัสดีครับ | sawatdi khrap | sà-wàt-dii khráp | L-L-M + H | hello (male speaker) | Standard male greeting. |
| สวัสดีค่ะ | sawatdi kha | sà-wàt-dii khâ | L-L-M + F | hello (female speaker) | Standard female greeting. Statement → falling-tone ค่ะ. |
| หวัดดี | watdi | wàt-dii | L-M | clipped "sawatdi" | Informal, friends/peers only. Very common in speech and chat (also spelled หวัดดีครับ/ค่ะ with particles for light politeness). Do NOT use to elders/officials. |
| ดี (chat: ดีจ้า) | di | dii | M | "good" | Ultra-casual "hi" among close friends, mostly texting. Mention only as recognition item. |
| อรุณสวัสดิ์ | arun sawat | à-run sà-wàt | L-M-L-L | "dawn + well-being" = good morning | Literary/broadcast calque of "good morning". Thais rarely say it in daily life — teach for recognition only; สวัสดี covers all times of day. |
| ราตรีสวัสดิ์ | ratri sawat | raa-trii sà-wàt | M-M-L-L | "night + well-being" = good night | Same status: bookish/romantic, not everyday speech. |

**Etymology of สวัสดี (well-sourced, good "story" material for Lesson 1):**
- Root: Sanskrit *svasti* (स्वस्ति) "well-being, good fortune, auspiciousness" — same root as *svastika*
  "auspicious thing". Before the 1930s the word existed in Thai only as a formulaic auspicious opening
  in inscriptions/texts, not as a greeting.
- Coined as a greeting by **Phraya Upakit Silapasan** (พระยาอุปกิตศิลปสาร, 1879–1941), Thai-language
  scholar at the Faculty of Arts, **Chulalongkorn University**, in **BE 2476 ≈ 1933/34** (some sources
  say loosely "mid-1930s"). He introduced it among his students; it spread partly via radio.
- **Officially promoted/adopted by the Thai government in 1943** under Prime Minister Plaek
  Phibunsongkhram as the standard national greeting (part of the era's state cultural mandates).
- So "sawatdi" is an *invented* greeting less than a century old — Nation Thailand ran a piece titled
  "Sawatdee: The invented Thai greeting". Before it, Thais greeted phatically ("Where are you going?",
  "Have you eaten?") — see §4.
- ⚠ Minor source variance: coining year appears as 1933, 1934, or "mid-1930s". Safe phrasing for
  lessons: "coined in the early-to-mid 1930s at Chulalongkorn University, made official in 1943."

**Tone of สวัสดี — verified:** dictionary syllabification is สะ-หฺวัด-ดี. สะ = high-class ส + short
dead syllable → LOW; หฺวัด = ว with leading ส behaving as high-class + dead syllable → LOW; ดี =
mid-class ด + live long syllable → MID. So **sà-wàt-dii = Low–Low–Mid**.
⚠ CONFLICT: one search-result source called the first syllable "mid". Resolution: standard analysis is
LOW; the first syllable is an unstressed reduced syllable in fast speech, so its tone is barely audible
(and colloquially the whole syllable drops → หวัดดี). Teach L-L-M; tell students the first syllable is
light and quick.

### 1.2 Polite particles (spoken sentence-enders)

| Thai | RTGS | Phonetic | Tone | Who / when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ครับ | khrap | khráp | H | Male speaker; ends statements AND questions (no tone change, one form for everything). Colloquially reduced to kháp (r dropped). |
| ครับผม | khrap phom | khráp-phǒm | H-R | Emphatic/deferential male "yes sir / certainly". Recognition item. |
| ค่ะ | kha | khâ | **F (falling)** | Female speaker; ends STATEMENTS, acknowledgements ("yes"), thanks, greetings: สวัสดีค่ะ, ขอบคุณค่ะ. |
| คะ | kha | khá | **H (high)** | Female speaker; ends QUESTIONS and softened requests: สบายดีไหมคะ. Also after นะ → นะคะ. |
| นะครับ / นะคะ | na khrap / na kha | ná khráp / ná khá | H+H / H+H | Softener "…okay?/you know" added before the particle; makes sentences gentler. |
| จ้ะ | cha | jâ | F | Warm/intimate statement particle (to children, close friends, juniors); female-associated but used by both. |
| จ๊ะ | cha | já | H | Question counterpart of จ้ะ among intimates. |
| จ้า | cha | jâa | F (long) | Drawn-out friendly acknowledgement, very common in chat ("okay~", "yes dear"). |
| ฮะ / ฮ่ะ | ha | há / hâ | H / F | Casual male substitutes for ครับ (teen/младший register); ฮะ also = "huh?". ⚠ one source transcribes ฮ่ะ as "hà"; by orthographic rule ฮ + mai ek = falling (hâ). Flag as low-confidence; recognition only. |

**ค่ะ vs คะ — the critical fact (verify status: SOLID):**
- ค่ะ = **falling tone** (low-class ค + mai ek → falling). Used for statements/answers.
- คะ = **high tone** (low-class ค + short dead syllable, no mark → high). Used for questions/requests.
- This is inverted relative to what the spelling suggests to learners (the marked one is the
  "plain-feeling" statement form), and mixing them up is the single most cited female-learner error —
  Thais report it sounds strange/amusing, and it can leave listeners unsure whether you asked or stated.
- ⚠ CONFLICT: one source (Speekeo summary) called คะ "rising tone". Majority of sources (ThaiToYou,
  StudyThai.ai, Thailand Foundation, into-asia) and the orthographic rule say HIGH. Resolution: HIGH.
  The confusion arises because the Thai high tone phonetically rises toward the end (≈45), so casual
  writers mislabel it "rising". Lessons must say "high tone", optionally noting it "rises from high".
- Men have it easy: ครับ is high tone in ALL sentence types.
- Frequency guidance: particles are not needed on literally every sentence in a long exchange, but
  beginners should attach them to every learned formula (greeting, thanks, name, goodbye) — omitting
  them entirely reads as abrupt/rude (see §6 mistake #7).
- Note for writers: these particles are gendered by *speaker*, not by addressee. (Gay/kathoey speakers
  may use ค่ะ; do not build this into Lesson 1 beyond a footnote if desired.)

## 2. Grammar and Structure Notes

### 2.1 The polite-particle system (the real "grammar" of Lesson 1)
1. Particle choice is governed by **speaker's gender** (not addressee's): ครับ (M), ค่ะ/คะ (F).
2. For women, particle form is additionally governed by **sentence type**: statement → ค่ะ (falling);
   question/request → คะ (high). Men: ครับ everywhere.
3. Particles attach to the very end of the utterance, after everything else, incl. นะ: นะครับ / นะคะ.
4. Particles alone work as responses: "ครับ" / "ค่ะ" = "yes / mm-hm / I hear you" (also how you answer
   when your name is called, and how you say "hello" answering the phone after ฮัลโหล).
5. Register ladder (high → low): ครับ~ค่ะ/คะ → ฮะ (casual M) / จ้ะ~จ๊ะ~จ้า (intimate) → nothing (blunt).

### 2.2 The pronoun system (minimum viable set for Lesson 1)
- I (m) = ผม; I (f, everyday) = ฉัน; I (f, formal) = ดิฉัน; you (polite) = คุณ; we = เรา.
- Thai routinely **drops pronouns** once context is clear: ชื่อแนนค่ะ "(My) name is Naen." Teach the
  full pattern first, then show the natural pro-drop version.
- Thais often use **names/kin terms instead of pronouns** (calling yourself by your nickname; calling
  an older person พี่ phîi "older sibling", a younger one น้อง nɔ́ɔng). Full honorific system is out of
  scope for L1; introduce only พี่/น้อง as a cultural preview and คุณ + first name for addressing.
- Warning for writers: pronoun choice encodes gender + politeness simultaneously; wrong pronoun is a
  bigger social error than wrong tone. Keep the L1 inventory to the 5 above.

### 2.3 Sentence patterns for introductions
```
[PRONOUN] + ชื่อ + [NAME] + (ครับ/ค่ะ)          ผมชื่อจอห์นครับ    "I'm called John."
ชื่อเล่น + ชื่อ + [NICKNAME] + (ครับ/ค่ะ)        ชื่อเล่นชื่อเบสท์ค่ะ  "(My) nickname is Best."
คุณ + ชื่อ + อะไร + (ครับ/คะ)                  คุณชื่ออะไรคะ       "What's your name?" (f asker → คะ!)
ยินดีที่ได้รู้จัก + (ครับ/ค่ะ)                    — "Nice to meet you."   Reply: เช่นกัน + particle
สบายดีไหม/มั้ย + (ครับ/คะ) → สบายดี + (ครับ/ค่ะ)
```
Key structural facts: no copula "is" needed with ชื่อ (it's verbal: "to be named"); no verb
conjugation; no articles; question word อะไร stays **in place** at the end (no inversion) and ไหม/มั้ย
turns statements into yes-no questions with zero word-order change. These are gifts to all four L1
groups — say so explicitly to offset tone anxiety.

## 3. The Wai (ไหว้, wâi, falling tone) and Body Language

### 3.1 The three levels (as standardized by the Thai Ministry of Culture)
| Level | Hands | For whom |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (standard) | Palms together at **chest**, fingertips ~neck level; slight head bow | Peers, equals, returning a wai |
| 2 (respect) | Fingertips at **chin/nose** level; deeper bow | Parents, teachers, elders, bosses, social superiors |
| 3 (highest) | **Thumbs between the eyebrows**, index fingertips at forehead | Monks, royalty, Buddha images, sacred objects |

### 3.2 Rules of engagement
- **The junior/lower-status person initiates.** The senior chooses how (or whether) to respond, and
  returns it at a *lower* hand position or with a nod/smile (รับไหว้ ráp wâi "receive the wai").
- **Monks do not return a wai** to laypeople. Adults generally do not wai children.
- Thais do NOT wai people younger/лower in the interaction hierarchy — including **service staff**
  (taxi drivers, waiters, hotel staff, 7-Eleven clerks). Staff will wai the customer first; the correct
  response is a smile, nod, and verbal ขอบคุณครับ/ค่ะ — not a full formal wai back.
- Foreigners are not strictly expected to initiate wais, but **returning one is always appreciated**.
  When in doubt: smile + slight nod + verbal greeting is never wrong.
- Accompaniments: slight bow of head/shoulders; women may dip slightly. Speak the greeting while
  wai-ing: สวัสดีครับ/ค่ะ + wai is the full package.

### 3.3 Common foreigner wai mistakes (rich material for a "don't do this" box)
1. **Over-wai-ing** — wai-ing every bellhop and vendor forces them into awkward status repair.
2. Wai-ing at the **wrong height** (forehead-level wai to a waiter reads as parody or confusion).
3. Wai-ing **while holding things** / phone in hand — put items down or nod instead.
4. Treating it as a handshake substitute to be initiated at strangers of visibly lower status.
5. Ignoring a wai offered to you (respond with at least a nod + smile).

### 3.4 Related body language for greetings context
- **Head = sacred**: never touch anyone's head (even children — pat shoulders instead, and even that
  sparingly with adults).
- **Feet = lowest**: don't point feet at people/Buddha images, don't step over people.
- Handshakes exist in business but the wai remains default; let the Thai party offer a hand first.
- Physical greeting contact (hugs, cheek kisses) is not part of Thai greeting culture.

## 4. Cultural Context

### 4.1 Nickname culture (ชื่อเล่น)
- Virtually **every Thai has a nickname**, given in childhood, used by family, friends, colleagues —
  often the legal first name is used only in documents/school rolls.
- Why: legal names (ชื่อจริง chʉ̂ʉ-jing) are long, Sanskrit/Pali-derived, chosen for auspiciousness
  (e.g., Thanaporn, Suchanun); nicknames are 1–2 syllables and often have **no connection** to the
  legal name.
- Nickname sources: animals (หมู Muu "pig", กบ Gòp "frog"), sizes (เล็ก Lek "small"), fruit, and a huge
  modern layer of English loans: Bank, Best, Boss, Fern, Ice, Bee, First, New…
- Practical teaching point: when a Thai person says "My name is X", X is usually the nickname; asking
  ชื่อเล่นชื่ออะไร is friendly, normal, and expected. Foreign students should pick/accept a short Thai
  nickname — it's an instant rapport builder.
- Address = คุณ + first name or nickname (Khun Somchai, Khun Best). **Never surname** (no "Khun
  Thaitrong") — Thais do not address by family name. Surnames themselves are a 20th-century
  introduction and are legally quasi-unique per family, which is why they're so long.

### 4.2 กินข้าวหรือยัง and phatic greetings
- Before สวัสดี was invented (1930s, §1.1), Thais greeted with practical-warmth questions, and these
  are STILL the real everyday greetings among acquaintances:
  - กินข้าวหรือยัง "Eaten rice yet?" — expresses care; food is central to Thai social life.
  - ไปไหน bpai nǎi "Where are you going?" / ไปไหนมา "Where have you been?"
- Critical cultural decoding for students: these are **phatic** — like English "How's it going?".
  กินข้าวหรือยัง is *not* (usually) a meal invitation, and ไปไหน is *not* nosy interrogation; the literal
  answer barely matters. Standard replies: กินแล้ว "already ate" / ยัง "not yet"; for ไปไหน, a vague
  answer or smile suffices.
- สบายดีไหม is real but somewhat textbook-flavored; Thais use it more with people they haven't seen
  for a while than as a daily hello.

### 4.3 Hierarchy and the smile
- Thai society organizes interactions by **age and status**: who wais first, which pronouns/particles
  appear, who is พี่ (older sibling / senior) vs น้อง (junior). Deference to elders is baseline
  politeness, not optional formality.
- The **Thai smile** is a multifunctional social instrument — greeting, thanks, apology, embarrassment
  defusal, even disagreement. (Travel sources claim "13 named varieties of Thai smile" — colorful but
  soft/anecdotal; use as a sidebar, not a fact test.) Lesson takeaway: smile while greeting; a smile +
  nod outranks a clumsy wai.
- "Face" (การรักษาหน้า): avoid public confrontation, loud voices, and correcting people publicly —
  relevant even in Lesson 1 role-plays (model gentle, smiling interactions).

### 5.3 Per-L1 difficulty analysis

**A. English speakers (hardest overall path)**
- *Tones*: all five are new as lexical features. Specific traps in this lesson: English speakers put
  question intonation (rise) on statements → accidentally converts ค่ะ toward คะ; greeting intonation
  ("Hello!" with fall-rise) mangles สวัสดี. The rising tone (ผม, ไหม) is easiest to explain ("like a
  one-word question: 'What?'"); the low vs mid contrast is hardest to hear.
- *Consonants*: the killer is **unaspirated ป /p/, ต /t/, จ /tɕ/** vs aspirated พ, ท, ช — English has
  no unaspirated voiceless stops word-initially. In this lesson: ไปก่อน (bpai), เป็น in ไม่เป็นไร.
  Sources rank the aspiration/vowel-length gaps as causing MORE communication failure than tones.
- *Finals*: Thai final stops are **unreleased** — ครับ ends with lips closed, no audible "p-puff";
  หวัด in สวัสดี has an unreleased t (why tourists hear "sawa-dee"). English speakers release finals.
- *Vowel length*: ชื่อ chʉ̂ʉ long + the vowel /ʉ/ itself has no English equivalent (say "ee" with
  spread lips, tongue further back).
- *Other*: ครับ's /r/ — reassure students Thais themselves mostly say kháp.

**B. Mainland-China Mandarin speakers**
- *Head start*: tonal L1 → faster tone acquisition (research: tonal-L1 learners acquire new tone systems
  faster; Chinese learners reach milestones in ~70–80% of the time English speakers need).
- *Tone mapping (analysis; pitch values standard)* — teach as "similar / adjust / new":
  | Thai tone | Mandarin anchor | Adjustment needed |
  |---|---|---|
  | Falling (51) | Tone 4 (51) | Nearly free transfer — best friend. Use for ค่ะ, ชื่อ, ไม่. |
  | Mid (33) | Tone 1 (55) | LOWER it — Thai mid sits mid-range, not high-level. Classic error: ดี said like 滴 dī sounds high, drifting toward Thai high tone. |
  | Rising (14) | Tone 2 (35) | Start LOWER and rise later/further (research note: Thai rising starts below Mandarin's). |
  | Low (21) | (half tone 3, 21) | **No true equivalent** — research (ERIC EJ1280917) confirms the low tone stays hardest for Chinese students; anchor to the first half of tone 3 without the final rise. |
  | High (45) | (—) | No equivalent; NOT tone 1 (level) and not tone 2 (rises from mid). It's high with a late upward kick — drill with คะ vs ค่ะ. |
- *Finals*: Mandarin allows only -n/-ng finals → the unreleased **-p -t -k** (ครับ, หวัด, จัก) and final
  **-m** (ผม) are new. Typical errors: deleting the final, releasing it, or appending a vowel
  ("khrap-uh"); for -m, substituting -n (ผม → *phǒn). Teach "close the mouth, kill the sound".
- *Onsets*: Mandarin b/d/g are already unaspirated voiceless = exactly Thai ป/ต/ก, and p/t/k aspirated =
  พ/ท/ค → aspiration contrast transfers beautifully. NEW: Thai บ /b/ ด /d/ are *truly voiced* — a
  three-way contrast (บ-ป-พ) where Mandarin has two; expect บ/ป merger (ไม่เป็นไร, บ๊ายบาย).
- *Romanization*: Pinyin habits distort RTGS reading (RTGS "kh" ≠ pinyin k; RTGS "p" = pinyin b!).
  Give the pinyin equivalences explicitly.

**C. Hong Kong Cantonese speakers (best-positioned group — say so, it motivates)**
- *Tones*: 6 lexical tones incl. **level tones at three heights** → hearing Thai mid vs low level
  distinctions is far easier than for Mandarin speakers. Mapping (analysis, standard values):
  Thai mid 33 ≈ C-T3 (33); Thai low 21 ≈ C-T4 (21) — the tone Mandarin speakers struggle with most is
  nearly free for Cantonese speakers; Thai rising 14 ≈ C-T5→T2 territory (23/25); Thai high 45 ≈ C-T2
  (25/35) high-rising. The one genuine gap: **Thai falling (51)** — modern HK Cantonese has no
  high-falling tone (the old 53 variant of T1 has merged to 55 for most speakers), so ค่ะ/ชื่อ/ไม่
  need explicit contour drilling. Flag: this mapping is researcher analysis; no ready-made
  Cantonese→Thai tone-pairing source was found.
- *Finals*: Cantonese has the complete set **-p -t -k (unreleased!) -m -n -ng** — ครับ, ผม, หวัด
  transfer perfectly. Huge advantage; celebrate it.
- *Onsets*: like Mandarin, Cantonese has aspirated vs unaspirated voiceless (b=/p/, p=/pʰ/) → ป/พ fine,
  but true voiced บ/ด still new. **No /r/** in Cantonese → ครับ and รู้จัก need work (l-for-r
  substitution is the expected error; note Thais' own r→l casualness as comfort, but รู้ rúu with /l/
  becomes ลู้ — drill it).
- *Vowels*: Cantonese has /œ/y/ front-rounded vowels → เจอ jəə is approachable; /ʉ/ in ชื่อ still new.

**D. Taiwan Mandarin speakers**
- Everything in (B) applies — same core language. Differences worth writing into the TW variant:
  - Taiwanese Mandarin **tone 3 is typically realized as a plain low-falling (21) without the final
    rise** → an even cleaner anchor for the Thai low tone than Mainland "half tone 3". Potential
    *advantage* over Mainland learners on Thai's hardest tone (analysis; hedge as "often noted").
  - Many Taiwanese also speak **Taiwanese Hokkien (7–8 tones, full -p -t -k -m checked finals)** —
    Hokkien-speaking students get the Cantonese-style finals advantage; a Threads/teaching source
    explicitly markets Thai tones as easy for Taiwanese ("華語有四聲，台語更有八聲"). An NTNU master's
    thesis exists on beginner Thai pedagogy for Taiwanese learners (culture-integrated approach).
  - Romanization interference differs: many Taiwanese learned **Zhuyin (ㄅㄆㄇ)**, not Pinyin — Zhuyin's
    ㄅ/ㄆ split actually maps cleanly onto ป/พ; give Zhuyin equivalences in the TW lesson, not Pinyin.
  - Taiwan Mandarin merges retroflex/dental (zh→z etc.) — irrelevant to this lesson's inventory; ignore.

## 6. Common Learner Mistakes (ranked by frequency × social cost)

1. **ค่ะ/คะ swap (female learners)** — saying falling ค่ะ at the end of a question (สบายดีไหมค่ะ ✗) or
   high คะ after a statement (สวัสดีคะ ✗ — extremely common even in Thai social-media typos).
   Correction: statement→ค่ะ(F), question→คะ(H); after นะ always คะ.
2. **Gender-swapped particles/pronouns** — men saying ค่ะ or women ครับ (usually from copying a
   teacher/partner of the other gender). High amusement factor for listeners; drill from day one:
   particle follows the SPEAKER's gender.
3. **Flat or English-intonation สวัสดี** — losing L-L-M, especially adding a final rise. Correction
   drill: sà-wàt low and clipped, dii level and long.
4. **Over-wai-ing / wrong wai height** — wai-ing waiters and 7-Eleven staff, or forehead-wai for
   ordinary adults. Correction: smile + nod + ขอบคุณครับ/ค่ะ for service; chest-level for peers.
5. **ลาก่อน as everyday goodbye** — sounds like a permanent farewell/breakup. Correction: แล้วเจอกัน,
   เจอกัน, บ๊ายบาย, or ไปก่อนนะ.
6. **Released/dropped final stops** — "khrap" with an English p-puff (E) or "khra" with lost final
   (CN-Mandarin). Correction: close the articulator, no release; for ผม end with closed lips (-m not -n).
7. **Omitting particles entirely** — grammatical but abrupt; beginner formula = phrase + particle,
   always.
8. **Using ดิฉัน in casual talk (women)** — stiff/over-formal; use ฉัน (or drop the pronoun).
9. **Misreading กินข้าวหรือยัง / ไปไหน as literal** — answering with meal plans or itineraries, or
   thinking it's an invitation. Correction: กินแล้ว/ยัง and move on.
10. **Khun + surname** ("Khun Smith") — Thais address by first name/nickname; also using คุณ before
    one's own name.
11. **Translating time-of-day greetings** — อรุณสวัสดิ์ in speech sounds like a TV announcer; สวัสดี
    covers all times.
12. **Mandarin-speaker tone drift** — Thai mid said as Mandarin tone 1 (too high) and Thai low said as
    full tone 3 (with dip-rise). Correction cues in §5.3-B.

## 7. Romanization Policy (for all lesson writers)

- **RTGS (Royal Thai General System)**: official government system; ASCII-only, **no tone marks, no
  vowel length** — khao = ข้าว (rice, F) = เขา (he/she, R) = ข่าว (news, L). Ubiquitous on road signs
  and place names. Students WILL meet it in the wild → show it, but never as the pronunciation guide.
- **Paiboon(-style)**: learner system from Paiboon Publishing; tone diacritics (à â á ǎ, plain = mid),
  doubled letters for long vowels (aa, ʉʉ), and learner-friendly consonant choices (g for ก, bp/dt for
  ป/ต in some variants). This file uses Paiboon-style as its phonetic column.
- **Recommendation**: every vocab item in lessons carries three columns — Thai script, RTGS
  (recognition), Paiboon-style tone-marked phonetic (production). Add pinyin/zhuyin/jyutping hint
  columns in the CN/TW/HK variants per §5.3. Push learners toward script early; romanization is
  scaffolding (multiple sources note learners who stay on romanization plateau).

## 7b. Suggested Teaching Sequence (Lesson 1 flow)

1. **Hook**: สวัสดี is younger than your grandparents — invented 1930s, official 1943 (story from §1.1).
2. **Block A**: สวัสดี + ครับ/ค่ะ + chest-level wai (speak + gesture together). Drill L-L-M.
3. **Block B**: Names — ผม/ฉัน ชื่อ… + คุณชื่ออะไร(ครับ/คะ) → introduces the คะ question form
   naturally. Nickname culture sidebar; students pick Thai nicknames.
4. **Block C**: ยินดีที่ได้รู้จัก ↔ เช่นกัน; role-play a first meeting end-to-end.
5. **Block D**: สบายดีไหม/มั้ย → สบายดี / ไม่สบาย; contrast มั้ย(H)/ไม่(F) as the lesson's tone
   minimal pair; ค่ะ(F)/คะ(H) as the second.
6. **Block E**: Thanks + goodbyes: ขอบคุณ, ไม่เป็นไร, แล้วเจอกัน (NOT ลาก่อน), บ๊ายบาย.
7. **Culture capsule**: กินข้าวหรือยัง as "how are you"; wai dos/don'ts; smile.
8. **Per-L1 pronunciation clinic** (split content): E = unaspirated stops + tone intro; CN = low/mid
   tones + final -p/-t/-k/-m; HK = falling tone + /r/; TW = CN content with tone-3-as-low advantage +
   Zhuyin mapping + optional Hokkien finals shortcut.
