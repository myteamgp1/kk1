Welcome to your very first Thai lesson! You are about to say your first words in a language spoken by around 70 million people. Take a breath — we will go slowly, everything is explained from zero, and by the end of this page you will be able to greet a Thai person, introduce yourself, and say goodbye like a real human being (not a textbook). Let's begin. เริ่มกันเลย!

## 1. Lesson Goals (เป้าหมายบทเรียน)

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. Greet anyone with **สวัสดีครับ / สวัสดีค่ะ** and know why every Thai adds a little word at the end.
2. Introduce yourself and ask someone's name — **ผมชื่อ… / ฉันชื่อ… / คุณชื่ออะไร**.
3. Say "How are you?" and answer it, plus say thank you, sorry, and an *everyday* goodbye (not the dramatic one from old textbooks).
4. Produce the **5 Thai tones** consciously, and — the real prize for English speakers — hear and make the difference between "puffed" and "unpuffed" consonants (ป vs พ).
5. Perform a basic **wai** (the palms-together greeting) at the right height for the right person.

## 2. Orientation: How Thai Works (a 2-minute map before we drive)

This is Lesson #1, so instead of reviewing an earlier lesson, here is the shape of the whole language. Four pieces of very good news and one challenge.

**Good news 1 — No verb conjugation. Ever.** Thai verbs never change. "I go, he goes, they went, we will go" all use the same word ไป (bpai). Tense comes from little added words, not from mangling the verb. Compared to French or Spanish, this is a gift.

**Good news 2 — Word order is SVO, just like English.** Subject–Verb–Object. "I eat rice" = ผม กิน ข้าว (phǒm gin khâao) = literally "I eat rice." You already know the skeleton.

**Good news 3 — No articles, no plurals to worry about, no "to be" before adjectives.** "The cat is big" has no "the" and no "is." Thai strips out a lot of grammar you take for granted.

**Good news 4 — Questions don't reorder the sentence.** To ask a yes/no question you just add a question word at the end. No "do/does," no flipping. "You are well" + a tag = "Are you well?" Same word order.

**The challenge — Thai is a *tonal* language with 5 tones.** The pitch of your voice is part of the word, the same way a consonant is. Say the syllable *maa* with a low pitch and it means one thing; with a rising pitch it means another. In English pitch only shows emotion ("Really?" vs "Really."). In Thai, pitch changes the dictionary meaning. We'll drill this gently in §6 — for now just know it's real and it's learnable.

**One more thing you'll notice:** written Thai **has no spaces between words.** สวัสดีครับผมชื่อจอห์น is a whole sentence. Spaces appear only between phrases or sentences. Don't panic — this lesson spaces things out for you.

## 3. Core Vocabulary (คำศัพท์หลัก)

Every word below has four things: the **Thai script**, the **RTGS** spelling (the tone-less system you'll see on street signs — recognition only), the **Paiboon** phonetic spelling *with tone marks* (this is your pronunciation guide), and the **tone of each syllable** spelled out. Tone marks: plain vowel = mid, à = low, â = falling, á = high, ǎ = rising.

| Thai | RTGS | Paiboon (tones) | Tones | Meaning / usage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| สวัสดี | sawatdi | sà-wàt-dii | low–low–mid | Hello AND goodbye; works any time of day. Rarely said bare — add ครับ/ค่ะ. |
| ครับ | khrap | khráp | high | Politeness word said by **male** speakers, on statements *and* questions. |
| ค่ะ | kha | khâ | falling | Politeness word said by **female** speakers on **statements**. |
| คะ | kha | khá | high | Female politeness word on **questions**. (Yes — spelled differently, and this trips people up. See §7.) |
| ผม | phom | phǒm | rising | "I / me" — **male** speaker. (Also literally means "hair"!) |
| ฉัน | chan | chǎn | rising | "I / me" — **female**, everyday. |
| คุณ | khun | khun | mid | "you" (polite); also a title before a first name, like Mr/Ms. |
| ชื่อ | chue | chʉ̂ʉ | falling | "name" / "to be named" — works as a verb, so no "is" needed. |
| ชื่อเล่น | chue len | chʉ̂ʉ-lên | falling–falling | "nickname" (literally "play-name"). Every Thai has one. |
| อะไร | arai | à-rai | low–mid | "what" — stays at the **end** of the question. |
| สบายดีไหม | sabai di mai | sà-baai-dii mǎi | low–mid–mid–rising | "Are you well?" = How are you? |
| สบายดี | sabai di | sà-baai-dii | low–mid–mid | "I'm well / fine" — the standard answer. |
| ขอบคุณ | khop khun | khɔ̀ɔp-khun | low–mid | "thank you." |
| ไม่เป็นไร | mai pen rai | mâi-bpen-rai | falling–mid–mid | "no worries / you're welcome / it's nothing." The famous all-purpose phrase. |
| ขอโทษ | kho thot | khɔ̌ɔ-thôot | rising–falling | "sorry / excuse me." |
| แล้วเจอกัน | laeo choe kan | láeo-jəə-gan | high–mid–mid | "See you (later)" — the natural everyday goodbye. |
| บ๊ายบาย | bai bai | báai-baai | high–mid | "bye-bye" (from English), friendly and everywhere. |
| ยินดีที่ได้รู้จัก | yindi thi dai ru chak | yin-dii thîi dâai rúu-jàk | mid–mid–falling–falling–high–low | "Nice to meet you." Reply with เช่นกัน. |
| เช่นกัน | chen kan | chên-gan | falling–mid | "Likewise / same to you." |

**Recognition-only extras** (don't drill, just meet them): หวัดดี (wàt-dii, low–mid) casual "hi" among friends; ดิฉัน (dì-chǎn, low–rising) stiff formal "I" for women; ลาก่อน (laa-gɔ̀ɔn, mid–low) — the *dramatic* "farewell" we'll warn you about in §8.

## 4. Grammar Focus (ไวยากรณ์และโครงสร้าง)

### 4.1 The politeness particle — the real "grammar" of greetings

Thai attaches a tiny politeness word to the end of polite sentences. Which one you use depends on **your own gender** (not the listener's) and, for women, on **whether you're asking or telling**.

```
Men, always:                     … ครับ (khráp, high)
Women, making a statement:       … ค่ะ  (khâ, falling)
Women, asking a question:        … คะ  (khá, high)
```

Examples:
- สวัสดีครับ — sà-wàt-dii **khráp** — "Hello." (man speaking)
- สวัสดีค่ะ — sà-wàt-dii **khâ** — "Hello." (woman speaking, it's a statement)
- คุณชื่ออะไรคะ — khun chʉ̂ʉ à-rai **khá** — "What's your name?" (woman asking → high คะ)

**Note:** Omitting the particle entirely sounds abrupt, even rude. As a beginner, glue a particle onto every formula — greeting, thanks, name, goodbye. The particle alone (just "ครับ" / "ค่ะ") also means "yes / mm-hm / I'm listening."

### 4.2 Saying your name — no "is" required

Because ชื่อ (chʉ̂ʉ) already *means* "to be named," you don't need a "to be" verb.

```
[I-word] + ชื่อ + [name] + [particle]
```

- ผมชื่อจอห์นครับ — phǒm chʉ̂ʉ Jɔɔn khráp — "I'm called John." (man)
- ฉันชื่อแอนนาค่ะ — chǎn chʉ̂ʉ ʔɛɛn-nâa khâ — "I'm called Anna." (woman)
- ชื่อเล่นชื่อบอสค่ะ — chʉ̂ʉ-lên chʉ̂ʉ Bɔ́ɔt khâ — "My nickname is Boss." (woman)

**Note — pronoun dropping:** Once it's clear who you mean, Thai loves to drop the "I." ชื่อแอนนาค่ะ ("Name Anna, [particle]") is completely natural. Learn the full pattern first, then feel free to trim.

### 4.3 Asking a question — just add a tag, don't reorder

Thai keeps the word order of a statement and tacks a question word onto the end.

```
Yes/no question:   [statement] + ไหม / มั้ย + [particle]
"What" question:   … + อะไร (stays at the end) + [particle]
```

- สบายดีไหมครับ — sà-baai-dii **mǎi** khráp — "Are you well?" (Notice: no "do," no flipping.)
- คุณชื่ออะไรครับ — khun chʉ̂ʉ **à-rai** khráp — "What's your name?" (à-rai sits at the end.)
- คุณมาจากไหนคะ — khun maa-jàak nǎi khá — "Where are you from?"

**Note:** In writing you'll see ไหม (mǎi, rising). In real speech people usually say it as มั้ย (mái, **high**). Learn to read ไหม, and to say มั้ย.

## 5. Dialogues (บทสนทนา)

### Dialogue A — Two people meet for the first time (Anna, a woman; Somchai, a man)

1. **Somchai:** สวัสดีครับ
   sà-wàt-dii khráp
   "Hello."

2. **Anna:** สวัสดีค่ะ
   sà-wàt-dii khâ
   "Hello."

3. **Somchai:** ผมชื่อสมชายครับ คุณชื่ออะไรครับ
   phǒm chʉ̂ʉ Sǒm-chaai khráp, khun chʉ̂ʉ à-rai khráp
   "I'm Somchai. What's your name?"
   *(word-for-word: I / named / Somchai / [particle], you / named / what / [particle])*

4. **Anna:** ฉันชื่อแอนนาค่ะ ชื่อเล่นชื่อแอนค่ะ
   chǎn chʉ̂ʉ ʔɛɛn-nâa khâ, chʉ̂ʉ-lên chʉ̂ʉ ʔɛɛn khâ
   "I'm Anna. My nickname is Ann."

5. **Somchai:** ยินดีที่ได้รู้จักครับ
   yin-dii thîi dâai rúu-jàk khráp
   "Nice to meet you."

6. **Anna:** เช่นกันค่ะ
   chên-gan khâ
   "Likewise."

### Dialogue B — Two friends run into each other (casual, then a warm goodbye)

1. **Bee (woman):** หวัดดีจ้า สบายดีไหม
   wàt-dii jâa, sà-baai-dii mǎi
   "Hi! How are you?"
   *(word-for-word: hi / [warm particle], well / [question tag])*

2. **Ice (man):** สบายดีครับ แล้วคุณล่ะ
   sà-baai-dii khráp, láeo khun lâ
   "I'm good. And you?"

3. **Bee:** สบายดีค่ะ กินข้าวหรือยัง
   sà-baai-dii khâ, gin khâao rʉ̌ʉ yang
   "I'm fine. Have you eaten yet?"
   *(word-for-word: eat / rice / or / yet)*

4. **Ice:** กินแล้วครับ
   gin láeo khráp
   "Already eaten."

5. **Bee:** โอเค ไปก่อนนะ แล้วเจอกัน
   ʔoo-khee, bpai gɔ̀ɔn ná, láeo jəə gan
   "Okay, I'm off — see you!"

6. **Ice:** บ๊ายบายครับ โชคดีครับ
   báai-baai khráp, chôok-dii khráp
   "Bye-bye! Good luck / take care."

> **Culture note inside the dialogue:** In turn 3, *"Have you eaten yet?"* is **not** an invitation to lunch. It's a warm, phatic greeting — the Thai equivalent of "How's it going?" Just answer กินแล้ว ("ate already") or ยัง ("not yet") and move on. More in §8.

## 6. Pronunciation Notes — for English speakers (เจาะลึกการออกเสียง)

Here is the honest truth that most beginner materials bury: **for English speakers, the hardest part of Thai is not the tones — it's the consonants.** Specifically, the difference between "puffed" and "unpuffed" sounds. Mispronouncing these causes more real misunderstandings than tones do. So we tackle them *first*.

### 6.1 Aspiration: the "puff of air" that English hides

Hold your palm in front of your mouth and say **"pot."** You feel a puff of air on the *p* — that's **aspiration**. Now say **"spot."** The *p* has *no* puff. English has both sounds, but you never noticed, because to you they're "the same p." **In Thai they are two completely different letters.**

- **พ / ท / ค** = *aspirated* — WITH the puff. Like the *p* in "**p**ot", *t* in "**t**op", *k* in "**k**ite."
- **ป / ต / ก** = *unaspirated* — NO puff. Like the *p* in "s**p**ot", *t* in "s**t**op", *k* in "s**k**ate."

The trick: say the English word with the *s*, then chop off the *s* in your mind. "**s**pot" → "pot-with-no-puff" = the Thai ป sound. Practice with your palm up — if the paper/hand doesn't move, you nailed the unaspirated one.

In this lesson: **ไปก่อน** (bp**ai** gɔ̀ɔn, "go first") and **เป็น** in **ไม่เป็นไร** both start with unaspirated ป — think "sp" with no s. We even write it "bp" to remind you it's softer than an English "p."

### 6.2 Final consonants are *unreleased* — stop, don't pop

When ครับ (khráp) ends in *p*, your lips close and… stop. No little "puh" at the end. English speakers pop their final consonants ("khrap-puh"); Thai swallows them. This is exactly why tourists hear "sà-wat-DEE" — the *t* in wàt is closed but never released. Practice: say "khráp" and freeze with your lips shut.

*(Bonus reassurance: the *r* in ครับ is barely pronounced — most Thais just say "kháp." You have permission to do the same.)*

### 6.3 The 5 tones — from absolute zero

Pitch = part of the word. Here are all five, with a way to *feel* each one:

| # | Tone | What your voice does | Feel it like… | Word in this lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **mid** | flat, at your normal speaking pitch | a calm, steady "aah" at the doctor | ดี dii, คุณ khun |
| 2 | **low** | flat but *below* your normal pitch | a low, quiet "oh" | ขอบ khɔ̀ɔp, สะ- in สวัสดี |
| 3 | **falling** | start high, drop down | a disappointed "oh **no**…" falling away | ค่ะ khâ, ชื่อ chʉ̂ʉ, ไม่ mâi |
| 4 | **high** | pitched high, pushing up at the end | an excited, sharp "yay!" | คะ khá, ครับ khráp |
| 5 | **rising** | dip down then swoop up | a surprised, questioning "**what?**" | ผม phǒm, ไหม mǎi |

The **rising** tone is your friend — just say the word like a one-word question ("What?!"). The **falling** tone is like sighing "no." The hardest pair to *hear* is **mid vs low** — they're both flat, one just sits lower. Don't stress; get mid and low "in the right neighborhood" and you'll be understood.

**Watch your English habits:** English puts a *rising* pitch on questions and a *fall-rise* on greetings. If you say "sà-wàt-diiii?" with a friendly upward swoop, you've bent the tones out of shape. สวัสดี is **low–low–mid**: the first two syllables low and clipped, ดี level and long. Keep it flat and calm.

## 7. Common Mistakes — English speakers (ข้อผิดพลาดที่พบบ่อย)

1. **❌ Puffing the unaspirated stops** — saying ไปก่อน with an English "p-puff" so it sounds like พ. **✅** Say "sp**ai**" then drop the s. No air on ป / ต / ก.
2. **❌ (Women) mixing up ค่ะ and คะ** — สวัสดีคะ (high, on a statement) or สบายดีไหมค่ะ (falling, on a question). **✅** Statement → **ค่ะ (falling)**, question → **คะ (high)**. Even Thais mistype this, but your ear will learn it fast.
3. **❌ Using the wrong-gender particle** — a man saying ค่ะ, a woman saying ครับ (very common when you copy a partner or teacher of the other gender). **✅** The particle follows **your** gender, always.
4. **❌ Singing สวัสดี with English intonation** — a cheerful upward "hellooo?" **✅** Low–low–mid, calm and level: sà-wàt-dii.
5. **❌ Popping final consonants** — "khrap-puh," "sà-wat-tee." **✅** Close the sound and stop; the final is unreleased.
6. **❌ Saying ลาก่อน (laa-gɔ̀ɔn) for a casual goodbye** — it sounds like "farewell forever." **✅** Use แล้วเจอกัน, บ๊ายบาย, or ไปก่อนนะ. (See §8.)

## 8. Culture Corner (มุมวัฒนธรรม)

### 8.1 สวัสดี is younger than your grandparents

Thailand's universal greeting was **invented in the early-to-mid 1930s** by a language scholar, **Phraya Upakit Silapasan**, at **Chulalongkorn University**, and made the official national greeting by the government in **1943**. Its root is the Sanskrit *svasti*, "well-being, good fortune" — the same root as *svastika*, "auspicious thing." Before สวัสดี existed, Thais greeted each other with warm, practical questions — which brings us to…

### 8.2 "Have you eaten yet?" is just "How are you?"

**กินข้าวหรือยัง** (gin khâao rʉ̌ʉ yang, "Eaten rice yet?") and **ไปไหน** (bpai nǎi, "Where are you going?") are genuine everyday greetings — but they're **phatic**, like the English "How's it going?" No one wants your meal plan or your itinerary. Just answer lightly (กินแล้ว "ate already" / ยัง "not yet") and smile. Reading these literally — and thinking you've been invited to lunch or interrogated — is a classic beginner misread.

### 8.3 The wai — palms together, and *height matters*

The **wai** (ไหว้, wâi, falling tone) is the palms-together greeting. Which height you use signals respect:

| Level | Hands | For whom |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | Palms together at the **chest**, fingertips ~neck | Peers, equals, returning a wai |
| Respect | Fingertips at the **chin/nose**, deeper bow | Elders, teachers, bosses |
| Highest | Thumbs **between the eyebrows** | Monks, royalty, Buddha images |

Rules that save you embarrassment:
- **The junior/lower-status person waits and initiates**; the senior returns it lower, or just nods.
- **Do not wai service staff** — taxi drivers, waiters, 7-Eleven clerks. They may wai you first; the correct reply is a **smile, a nod, and a spoken ขอบคุณครับ/ค่ะ**, not a formal wai back. Over-wai-ing forces staff into awkward status-repair.
- Don't wai with your **phone in your hand**; put things down or just nod.
- As a foreigner you're not *required* to initiate, but **returning** a wai is always appreciated. When unsure: **smile + slight nod + spoken greeting** is never wrong.

And two body rules that surprise visitors: the **head is sacred** (never pat anyone's head), and the **feet are lowest** (don't point them at people or Buddha images). A warm **smile** does an enormous amount of social work in Thailand — it can carry a greeting, a thank-you, and an apology all by itself.

## 10. New Student Track — "Just This For Today" (สำหรับนักเรียนใหม่)

If today feels like a lot, learn only this. Truly, this is enough to have a real first exchange:

**5 words:** สวัสดี (hello/bye) · ครับ/ค่ะ (your politeness word) · ชื่อ (name) · ขอบคุณ (thank you) · แล้วเจอกัน (see you).

**3 sentences you can say today:**
1. สวัสดีครับ / สวัสดีค่ะ — sà-wàt-dii khráp/khâ — "Hello."
2. ผมชื่อ… / ฉันชื่อ… — phǒm/chǎn chʉ̂ʉ … — "My name is…"
3. ขอบคุณครับ / ขอบคุณค่ะ — khɔ̀ɔp-khun khráp/khâ — "Thank you."

**Encouragement:** You do not need perfect tones to be understood today. A calm สวัสดี + a smile + the right politeness word will make any Thai person light up. You've already started. เก่งมาก! (gèng mâak — "well done!")

## 11. Returning Student Track — Stretch Goals (สำหรับนักเรียนเก่า)

Since this is Lesson #1, "returning" means: you got the basics above and want more. Try these.

- **Chain a full self-introduction** (5+ sentences): greet → name → nickname → where you're from (ผม/ฉัน มาจาก… maa-jàak…, "come from…") → nice to meet you. Record yourself and check every tone against §3.
- **Register-switch drill:** take Dialogue A (formal, with คุณ + ยินดีที่ได้รู้จัก) and rewrite it casually like Dialogue B (หวัดดี, จ้า, no ยินดี). Notice how the particles and vocabulary soften.
- **Minimal-pair ear training:** drill ไม่ (mâi, falling, "not") vs มั้ย (mái, high, question tag) — a real tone minimal pair. Then ค่ะ (khâ) vs คะ (khá). Have someone say one at random; identify which.
- **Pick your Thai nickname** (see §8-style culture): choose a short, friendly nickname and practice introducing yourself with ชื่อเล่นชื่อ… — this is exactly how Thais will address you, and it builds instant rapport.

## 12. Homework (การบ้าน) — ~20 minutes

You'll be asked about these in this evening's follow-up email, so give them a real try:

## Answer Key (เฉลย)

**Set A** — 1. ครับ · 2. ค่ะ (statement → falling) · 3. คะ (question → high!) · 4. ครับ · 5. ค่ะ.
*Why 3 is คะ and not ค่ะ:* it's a **question**, and women use the **high** คะ on questions. This is the #1 error to internalize.

**Set B** — 1-c · 2-d · 3-a · 4-b · 5-e.

**Set C** —
1. สวัสดีค่ะ — sà-wàt-dii khâ.
2. ผมชื่อมาร์คครับ — phǒm chʉ̂ʉ Máak khráp.
3. คุณชื่ออะไรคะ — khun chʉ̂ʉ à-rai khá. *(question → high คะ)*
4. สบายดีครับ ขอบคุณครับ — sà-baai-dii khráp, khɔ̀ɔp-khun khráp.
5. แล้วเจอกัน — láeo jəə gan (add ครับ/ค่ะ to be polite). Also fine: บ๊ายบาย.

**Set D** — The **aspirated** (puffed) one is always the second: 1. **พ** · 2. **ท** · 3. **ค**. The first of each pair (ป ต ก) has **no** puff — that's the sound English speakers must build from scratch.

**Set E** — **(b)** Smile, nod, and say ขอบคุณครับ/ค่ะ. You do **not** wai service staff back formally (and never at forehead height — that level is for monks and royalty); a handshake isn't the Thai norm here either. A warm smile + spoken thanks is exactly right.
