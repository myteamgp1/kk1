# Teaching Thai to Chinese-L1 Students (Taiwan · Mainland · Hong Kong)

Practical notes for lesson design and delivery. Teaching language is English;
use these contrasts to *explain*, and the students' L1 instincts to *accelerate*.

---

## 1. Tones — anchor to Mandarin, don't start from zero

Thai has 5 tones, Mandarin 4, Cantonese 6. Never teach Thai tones as if the
student had no tones; anchor them:

| Thai tone | Contour | Mandarin anchor | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mid (maa) | 33 flat | like T1 (媽 mā) **but lower and relaxed** | most common student error: saying it too high |
| Low (màa) | 21 low flat | like the *start* of T3 (馬) without the rise | "start low, stay low" |
| Falling (mâa) | 51 | ≈ T4 (罵 mà) | best 1:1 anchor — use it |
| High (máa) | 45 high (slightly rising in modern Bangkok speech) | no direct anchor; "T1 with tension, pushed up" | drill explicitly |
| Rising (mǎa) | 214→15 | ≈ T2 (麻 má), or full citation T3 | solid anchor |

Cantonese speakers: map mid/low to their level tones (33/22) — usually easy;
falling and rising need work.

**Always warn about the diacritic trap** (pinyin á = rising but Paiboon
á = high; pinyin à = falling but Paiboon à = low) — see
`reference/romanization.md` §1. Do this in the first 10 minutes of any
first lesson with a new student.

Drill order: *listen-and-identify before produce.* Minimal sets like
ข้าว khâao / ขาว khǎao / ข่าว khàao (rice/white/news) or
มา maa / หมา mǎa / ม้า máa (come/dog/horse).

## 2. Vowel length — the invisible problem

Mandarin and Cantonese have **no phonemic vowel length**; students won't hear
it until trained. Thai pairs differ by length alone:

- เข้า khâo (to enter) vs ข้าว khâao (rice) — same falling tone, length is the
  ONLY difference. Use this as the canonical drill pair.

Technique: exaggerate long vowels to 2× duration when modeling; have students
clap syllable length. Romanization shows it (single vs doubled letter) — point
at it every time.

## 3. Final stops -p -t -k (unreleased)

- **Mandarin speakers (Taiwan & mainland):** hardest single feature. Mandarin
  allows only -n/-ng finals. Students will add a vowel (rák → "rák-kə") or
  drop the stop entirely. Teach "close the mouth and stop the air — no puff."
- **Cantonese speakers:** free win — Cantonese has 入聲 -p/-t/-k finals
  (十 sap6, 一 jat1, 六 luk6). Tell them to reuse it; they'll be instantly good.
- **Taiwanese students who speak Hokkien/Taiwanese:** also have entering-tone
  finals (八 peh) — same free win, worth asking about.

## 4. Final -m

Mandarin has no -m final → students say -n instead (ต้ม dtôm → "dtôn").
Cantonese HAS -m (三 saam1) — no problem there. Drill: ต้ม dtôm / ต้น dtôn.

## 5. Aspiration — teach it as an advantage

Pinyin already encodes the exact contrast Thai needs:

| Pinyin habit | Thai equivalent |
|---|---|
| b (unaspirated) | ป bp |
| p (aspirated) | ผ พ ph |
| d (unaspirated) | ต dt |
| t (aspirated) | ถ ท th |
| g (unaspirated) | ก g |
| k (aspirated) | ข ค kh |

Thai adds a third row Chinese lacks: TRUE voiced บ b and ด d ("like English b/d,
with your throat buzzing"). Contrast drill: ปา bpaa / ผ้า phâa / บ้า bâa.

## 6. Sounds that don't exist in Mandarin

- **แอ ɛɛ** and **ออ ɔɔ**: students merge both into "o/e". Anchor ɔɔ to
  Cantonese 柯 (ngo) for HK students; for Mandarin speakers use English "law".
- **อึ/อือ ʉ**: no anchor anywhere — teach "say uu while smiling". High yield:
  it appears in มือ mʉʉ (hand), หนึ่ง nʉ̀ng (one), ครึ่ง khrʉ̂ng (half).
- **เออ əə**: close to Mandarin 呃/的 unstressed ə — easy once pointed out.
- **ng- at syllable START** (งู nguu, เงิน ngən): impossible-feeling for
  Mandarin speakers; natural for Cantonese (我 ngo5). Technique: say "singing",
  hold the "ng", drop everything before it.
- **r/l**: Thai ร is a tap/trill; colloquial Thai often merges it into l —
  teach standard forms, mention the colloquial reality so listening matches.
- **Clusters kr- kl- pr- pl-** (ปลา bplaa, กรุงเทพฯ): Chinese has no clusters;
  students insert a vowel ("bpa-laa"). Note colloquial Thai often drops the
  second element too (bplaa → bpaa) — teach careful form first.

## 7. Grammar — huge transfer advantages (say this out loud to students)

Thai and Chinese are typologically close; students should feel this as "easy":

- **No conjugation, no plurals, no articles** — same as Chinese ✓
- **SVO order** — same ✓
- **Classifiers**: Thai ตัว/คน/อัน ≈ Chinese 隻/個 measure words. Structure
  differs slightly: Thai = NOUN + NUMBER + CLF (หมา 2 ตัว), Chinese =
  NUMBER + CLF + NOUN (兩隻狗). Same concept, reversed order — one slide fixes it.
- **Final particles**: ครับ/ค่ะ/นะ/ล่ะ sit where 嗎/呢/吧/啊 sit — familiar feel.
  New: Thai politeness particles are **speaker-gender-based** (male ครับ khráp,
  female ค่ะ khâ / question คะ khá) — no Chinese equivalent, drill early.

## 8. Grammar — the two reliable interference errors

1. **Modifier order**: Thai modifiers FOLLOW the noun (บ้านใหญ่ "house big");
   Chinese precede (大房子). Expect 大房子-order errors for months; correct
   gently every time.
2. **Possessives**: Thai = thing + (ของ) + owner (บ้านของฉัน "house of me");
   Chinese = owner 的 thing (我的家). Same reversal as #1 — teach them together
   as one rule: "in Thai, the big thing comes first."

## 9. Classroom notes (teaching in English)

- Keep instruction English simple (CEFR B1-ish); students' English varies.
- Numbers: Thai numerals borrow from Chinese — สาม sǎam ≈ 三 sāam/sān,
  สี่ sìi ≈ 四, หก hòk ≈ 六 luk6 (Cantonese). Point it out; it makes numbers
  a 10-minute topic instead of a lesson.
- Writing Thai script: introduce gradually; handouts always show Thai script
  first so students absorb shapes passively, but never require reading it
  in a 1-hour topical lesson unless the topic IS the script.
- Cultural framing sells: students respond to street-food, travel, and
  workplace scenarios over textbook situations.
